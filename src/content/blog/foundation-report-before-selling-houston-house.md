**Before paying for foundation work on a Houston house you plan to sell, identify what your report actually answers. A home inspector's visual opinion, an engineering evaluation and a contractor's repair proposal are different documents.** Match the unresolved question to the right professional, then compare a defined repair plan with selling in the current condition.

A thick report is not automatically a complete diagnosis, and a short estimate is not automatically wrong. The important details are the scope, methods, observations, conclusions and exclusions. Start there before comparing dollar totals or promising a buyer that the foundation is “fixed.”

## Read the document's purpose before its conclusion

The [Texas Real Estate Commission's April 29, 2025 foundation guidance](https://www.trec.texas.gov/article/reporting-visible-and-present-indications-adverse-performance-foundation) explains that licensed inspectors provide a written performance opinion based on visible indications. Minimum requirements do not call for elevation equipment or load and deflection tests. TREC also distinguishes an inspector's role from structural engineering.

That does not make a visual inspection unhelpful. It tells you what the opinion is based on. If you need a different answer—such as the cause of a condition, a repair design or the significance of conflicting measurements—ask what additional evaluation is appropriate.

Do not translate “no adverse performance observed” into a promise that concealed components were examined. Likewise, a recommendation for further evaluation should not automatically become a fixed repair budget. Read the actual statement and its limits.

## Separate observations, analysis and a proposed project

An engineering document also has a scope. Do not assume it includes plumbing testing, every structural component or a repair design unless those services are stated. Ask the preparer to clarify an unfamiliar conclusion rather than trying to diagnose the house from a single number online.

Our existing [Houston foundation-problem page](/blog/sell-house-foundation-problems-houston/) explains the broader as-is evaluation path. This document review is a narrower step: deciding what evidence you need before authorizing a project or comparing offers.

## Build one dated foundation file

Gather previous reports, drawings, repair invoices, warranty terms, permit or inspection records where applicable, and photographs showing known changes. Keep dates attached. Separate old recommendations from work that was actually completed.

Write a simple timeline in plain language: what was observed, who evaluated it, what was proposed, what was completed and what changed afterward. Do not turn a contractor's verbal explanation into a verified engineering finding. If a record is missing, say so.

Read warranty transfer procedures directly. A seller should not advertise a transferable lifetime warranty merely because those words appear in an old brochure. Confirm the actual document, provider, covered work, deadlines, fees and exclusions before describing coverage to a buyer.

## Resolve conflicting reports before comparing repair prices

Two proposals can differ because they describe different work, not because one is necessarily dishonest. Compare the evaluated areas, access, proposed repair locations, drainage or plumbing assumptions, finish repairs, documentation and follow-up responsibilities.

For example, in a hypothetical sale, one estimate includes structural work while another includes structural work plus drainage changes and cosmetic restoration. Subtracting the totals without aligning scope produces a misleading comparison. Ask for a written explanation of what each project is intended to accomplish.

Do not use this guide to decide a house is safe or to perform testing yourself. If access or stability is in question, have an appropriate professional determine the next step and keep people away from unsafe areas.

## Compare repair-and-list with a current-condition proposal

A repair plan should include more than its initial quote: consider defined work, related restoration, carrying time and the uncertainty of the eventual selling price. An as-is offer should be evaluated using its written price, expenses, access, possession and contingencies.
