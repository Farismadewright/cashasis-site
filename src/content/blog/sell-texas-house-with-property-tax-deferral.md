**You can sell a Texas house with an existing homestead property tax deferral, but the deferral postponed collection and the tax lien is still attached to the property.** Before you accept an offer or schedule movers, ask the collector for the amount through your proposed closing date and have the title team show how it will be paid or cleared on the seller's closing statement.

## Start with the deferral record, then get the payoff

A homestead tax deferral and an exemption do different jobs. Under [Texas Tax Code Section 33.06](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/TX/htm/TX.33.htm#33.06), eligible owners who meet the residence homestead requirements can defer collection. The law keeps the tax lien in place and sets interest at 5% per year during the deferral or abatement. A deferral does not forgive the bill. This guide deals with an existing Section 33.06 deferral; different rules can apply to other tax deferral programs.

For Harris County, see the [Harris Central Appraisal District’s deferral guide](https://hcad.org/hcad-help/texas-first-time-property-owner/exemptions/tax-deferral-for-homeowners). For Fort Bend County, the [Fort Bend Central Appraisal District's deferral guide](https://www.fbcad.org/property-tax-deferrals-for-homeowners/) describes the affidavit and eligibility rules. Verify the appraisal district from the property record, especially if the mailing address says Houston or Missouri City. Then ask the collector shown on the account for the payment figures. The appraisal district is useful for finding the deferral record; your closing team needs the collector's figures to estimate what will be paid.

Give the closer the affidavit or confirmation, the years involved, and any notices. Ask for a breakdown of principal, interest and any other applicable charges, and confirm which taxing units are covered. Ask whether the current year's taxes will be handled as a separate proration; our [Houston tax proration guide](/blog/property-tax-proration-selling-houston-house/) explains the difference between a tax split and a past balance. You want the settlement statement to account for both without counting the same dollars twice.

## Don't use the collection window as a closing plan

Two dates matter: the date you stop using the property as your residence homestead, and the date the collector delivers the delinquency notice. Under Section 33.06(b), when the affidavit process applies, the restriction on new collection suits and tax-lien foreclosure sales runs until the 181st day after the collector delivers that notice following the change in ownership and occupancy described by the law. That is not an automatic 180 days from your closing date, and it does not tell you whether your title company can close without payment.

If you already moved out, give the collector and closer the move date and any notices now. If you have court papers, a scheduled tax sale, or questions about a surviving spouse's eligibility, take the documents to a Texas attorney promptly; don't use a general timeline to calculate a legal deadline. If you never filed a deferral and simply have overdue bills, start with the [Harris County back-tax guide](/blog/sell-house-back-taxes-harris-county/).

## Compare your proceeds before you pick a buyer

Ask both a listing agent and a cash buyer to estimate what you would keep after the tax payoff. A listing may reach more buyers and bring a higher price if you have room for showings and financing timelines. A cash buyer may suit a tighter move or a house you prefer to sell as-is, but the offer can be lower and the tax lien still needs attention.

**Hypothetical:** If a net sheet shows $76,000 after mortgage payoff and other seller expenses but leaves out a confirmed $8,400 deferred-tax payoff, the revised estimate is $67,600, assuming that payoff is charged to you and nothing else changes. Refresh the quote if closing slips. If the estimate is near zero or negative, ask the closer what would be required to close before promising a date.
