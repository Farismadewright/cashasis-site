**If you are unsure whether an addition to your Houston house was permitted, check the records before describing it as approved—or deciding it must be removed.** Missing paperwork, an issued permit and completed inspections are different things.

An enclosed porch or added room can create uncertainty when you want to sell. A useful response is a small evidence packet: what changed, which records exist, what remains unknown and who can answer the remaining questions.

This is a seller-preparation guide. The responsible permitting authority should explain permit status; qualified construction and real-estate professionals should address condition, disclosure and transaction-specific questions.

## Start with the property's actual jurisdiction

Confirm which authority handles the address before searching. A Houston mailing address is a starting point for locating the property, not a substitute for confirming jurisdiction.

For a City of Houston property, the City's [current permit-information page](https://www.houstontx.gov/planning/DevelopRegs/dev_reports.html) directs readers to Houston Public Works and the Sold Permits Search. It also explains that older permit-activity reports are reference archives rather than the source of current permit assistance.

Use the current guidance rather than treating an old neighborhood permit report as your house's complete construction history. If the property is outside City jurisdiction, ask the relevant city or county where to obtain its records.

## Ask for records that answer the actual question

Houston's [Public Information Act request directory](https://www.houstontx.gov/pia.html) lists building permits and building-code violations under Public Works. The City explains that a records request asks for existing information; it does not require the City to create a new analysis or answer legal questions.

That distinction helps you ask more precisely. Request available records associated with the addition and the property rather than asking a records clerk to certify that the house is legal to sell.

Keep an issued permit, an inspection result and a contractor invoice separately labeled. Each serves a different purpose. Ask the permitting authority to explain unclear status rather than combining the papers into an unsupported claim that everything passed.

### Write down unknowns instead of filling the gaps

Suppose, as a hypothetical example, an enclosed room existed when you bought the house. You have no construction date and cannot find a permit in your closing folder. Your useful statement is that the room predates your ownership and that you are gathering records. It is not proof that the work was approved, and it is not proof that it was prohibited.

If the search returns a record with a different project description, ask whether it relates to the addition. Do not assume an unrelated roof permit covers a converted porch.

## Decide on the next step before paying for corrective work

If the authority identifies an unresolved issue, ask what further information or professional assessment is needed. Obtain a scope and estimates before deciding that a major demolition or renovation is the only option. A records search alone cannot diagnose structural safety or establish the cost of a correction.

## Compare a repair-and-list plan with an as-is proposal

Resolving the issue before listing may be worthwhile if the work, timing and likely return are clear. Listing can expose the property to a wider buyer pool, but the preparation and uncertainty still belong in your comparison.

A cash buyer may consider the house in its current condition and account for unresolved work in the price. That does not confer permit approval or guarantee a closing.
