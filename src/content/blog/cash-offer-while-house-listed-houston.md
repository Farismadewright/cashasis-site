**A cash offer does not erase an existing listing agreement.** If your Houston house is already listed, first review that agreement with your broker, clarify any fee or release terms, and compare the money you would actually keep. Do that before signing a new purchase contract or promising a closing date.

This is a document check, not a reason to abandon either option. A listed house can attract a cash buyer, and continuing the listing may still be the best route. The question is whether the offer works alongside the commitments you already made.

## Separate the three decisions

Start with three different questions:

1. **Is this an offer worth considering?** Look at price, condition requirements, proposed timing and responsibilities.
2. **What does my broker agreement require?** Locate the signed listing, amendments and any later cancellation or release.
3. **What would remain at closing?** Include applicable broker compensation alongside payoff amounts and other seller costs.

These questions apply whether the property is in Houston, Harris County or Fort Bend County. The county name does not tell you what your privately negotiated broker agreement says.

For the first decision, our [cash-offer process guide](/blog/how-cash-offer-house-works-houston/) explains what property information helps a buyer evaluate the house. The additional step for an already-listed seller is resolving the broker side of the transaction.

## Pull the actual agreement, not just the listing screen

The [Texas Real Estate Commission explains that a listing contract is a private legal agreement](https://www.trec.texas.gov/i-listed-my-property-sale-broker-and-broker-has-done-nothing-about-selling-house-can-i-break-my). TREC does not decide private contractual questions. Its guidance identifies asking the broker for an agreed cancellation or release as one possible approach; a dispute about termination needs a private attorney's review.

Build a small packet before your conversation:

- The signed listing agreement and every amendment.
- The current term and any agreed changes to it.
- The compensation provisions and any provisions about a seller-found buyer.
- Any written cancellation, release, exclusions or remaining obligations.
- The cash proposal and any purchase contract you have already signed.

Removing a sign or changing an online listing status is not the document you need to rely on for this review. Ask the broker to identify the actual agreement governing the next step. If you already have a buyer under contract, disclose that immediately and get advice before committing the property again.

## Ask what happens to compensation in this specific sale

Avoid assuming that all Texas listing agreements have one fee structure. [Texas REALTORS' current compensation guidance](https://www.texasrealestate.com/legal-and-ethics/legal-resources/legal-faqs/listing-agreements-nar-settlement-and-forms-changes) describes negotiated arrangements, including percentage, flat-fee and alternative structures in its listing forms. Your signed version and later changes matter.

Use these questions with your broker:

- If this proposed buyer purchases the house, what compensation would apply under our agreement?
- Does that answer change if the buyer came through me rather than the listing?
- Would any change need a written amendment or release?
- Are there other agreed charges that should appear in the proceeds estimate?

CashAsIs does not charge you an agent commission as the direct buyer. **That does not waive a fee you may separately owe your own broker.** Our purchase agreement also should not be assumed to be the same form as your listing or a TREC resale contract.

## Check what remains after expiration or a release

Do not substitute “the listing ended” for reviewing the paperwork. [Texas REALTORS' listing-agreement FAQ](https://www.texasrealestate.com/legal-and-ethics/legal-resources/legal-faqs/listing-agreements) gives an example where a seller's termination agreement preserved a fee for a named buyer during a specified later period. That example illustrates a possible surviving term, not a rule that every seller owes the same fee or has the same deadline.

Ask whether the documents identify a protection period, particular buyers, notice requirements, exceptions or remaining charges. Have the broker explain which provisions apply to the proposed transaction. If the meaning is disputed, obtain your attorney's advice rather than guessing from a general article.

## Compare the two paths with the same cost categories

Make two planning columns: **continue the listing** and **accept the actual cash proposal**. In each, enter the proposed or assumed sale price, current payoff figures, broker fees that actually apply, other seller costs, agreed repairs or concessions, and expected holding costs.

Keep an unknown item marked “not confirmed.” Do not enter zero just because one proposal advertises fewer fees. The [net-proceeds comparison](/compare-the-net/) can organize your assumptions; it is not a closing statement or a determination of contractual liability.

Continued marketing may produce a higher price or a more suitable offer, particularly when you can accommodate showings and the property's condition. An as-is cash proposal may reduce repair preparation and simplify timing, but its price can be lower. Neither path guarantees a better net or a particular closing date.

Your next useful move is a coordinated conversation: share the proposal with your broker, resolve the agreement questions, and ask the closing team for an estimate based on the actual terms. You can then choose with the price, obligations and timing in view.
