**If a closing team asks about FIRPTA when you sell a Houston house, ask them to identify what they need to confirm and show any estimated withholding as a separate line on your seller net sheet.** Do this before comparing offers. A lower amount wired at closing can reflect money withheld for federal tax purposes, even when the agreed purchase price has not changed.

FIRPTA is the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act. The [IRS withholding guidance](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/firpta-withholding) explains that buyers generally have a withholding obligation on covered transfers of U.S. real property interests by foreign persons. The general rate is 15% of the amount realized where that rate applies. Exceptions and different treatment can apply. This is federal guidance; a house in Harris or Fort Bend County is subject to the same federal framework.

## Confirm who is selling before estimating the wire

Ask the closer which owner or entity is shown on the title documents and what information the closing team needs from each seller. Have a qualified tax professional confirm the relevant federal tax status. An overseas address, a remote signing plan, or the fact that one owner lives in Houston does not answer the question for every owner.

The [IRS exceptions page](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/exceptions-from-firpta-withholding) describes a nonforeign certification and certain buyer-residence exceptions, each with conditions. Do not sign a certification based on a guess or assume a price threshold alone qualifies your sale. Use the closing team's verified secure channel for taxpayer details. If the issue is where to sign rather than tax status, see our [remote closing guide](/blog/selling-houston-house-from-out-of-state-signing/).

## Separate the estimated withholding from final tax

Ask for an itemized estimate with the amount realized used for withholding, the basis for the amount or exception, and the resulting cash to you. The [IRS instructions for Form 8288](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8288) explain that amount realized can include more than cash alone, and describe how withheld tax can be credited. Do not apply a percentage to the amount left after paying off a mortgage. Your tax professional can explain the final reporting and what documents to retain.

**Hypothetical, not a tax estimate:** Assume a simple sale with an agreed price and amount realized of $200,000. If the closing team confirms that the general 15% rate applies and no exception or reduction is available, the withholding line is $30,000. If the projected proceeds before that line were $100,000 after other closing deductions, the estimate after withholding would be $70,000. Neither the $30,000 nor the $70,000 tells you what the final tax will be.

For another reason the headline amount can differ from money received, see our [Form 1099-S guide](/blog/form-1099-s-after-selling-houston-house/). That guide covers reporting after a sale; the withholding question belongs on the closing checklist earlier.

## Ask what has to be resolved before the sale

If a different withholding amount may apply, ask who is responsible for documenting it and what the closing team can confirm now. The IRS describes Form 8288-B as a way to apply for a withholding certificate that can reduce or eliminate withholding in appropriate cases. Do not budget for a lower amount or a specific closing date based only on a possible application.

When comparing a listed sale with a direct cash offer, use the [same net-proceeds categories](/compare-the-net/) for both. Listing can reach more buyers and may produce a higher price, with more time or preparation. A direct offer may reduce that work but can be lower, and it does not remove an applicable withholding obligation. Ask for a written offer and have the closing team and tax professional verify the proceeds assumptions before you decide.
