**Before accepting an offer on a Houston-area house, separate three questions: what mineral interest you own, what the contract would transfer or reserve, and what surface-use rights may remain.** Owning the house is not proof that you own every mineral interest beneath it. If the documents raise a mineral question, get it reviewed before you promise either to keep those rights or to include them in the price.

## Start with ownership rather than the offer price

The [Texas Railroad Commission explains the relationship between mineral and surface rights](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/about-us/faqs/oil-gas-faq/oil-gas-exploration-and-surface-ownership/). Surface and mineral interests can be owned separately. A reference to a reservation in an older deed is therefore a reason to investigate, not a number to assume belongs to the current seller.

Pull your deed, available earlier deeds or reservations, any known mineral lease, and relevant title documents into a packet for review. If royalties are involved, provide the related records through your attorney’s secure channel. A payment statement may help identify an issue; do not treat it as a complete ownership determination.

TREC’s [mineral reservation addendum, Form 44-3](https://www.trec.texas.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-forms/44-3.pdf), warns that mineral ownership requires a full title examination by an attorney experienced in the area. Ask the closing team what its ordinary title review covers and whether a separate mineral-title review is needed. ## Read what the buyer is actually asking for

TREC identifies its [reservation addendum](https://www.trec.texas.gov/forms/addendum-reservation-oil-gas-and-other-minerals-0) as a form for a seller reserving all or part of the mineral estate. It defines that estate broadly, including several rights and benefits beyond ownership of oil or gas. This is an example of a TREC form, not a statement that Cash As-Is uses it; the actual written agreement controls. Checking a reservation box is not a substitute for understanding which interest you own and which terms you are negotiating.

Before signing, ask your attorney to explain:

- Which interests the proposed contract includes and which, if any, you would keep.
- Whether an earlier reservation or existing lease affects the proposed terms.
- Whether the contract language, addendum and proposed deed carry out the same agreement.
- What supporting records or unresolved questions the buyer needs before committing to a closing date.

Do not add estimated future royalties to your proceeds unless the assumptions have been independently supported. A house price and the value of a separately retained mineral interest are different questions.

## Check surface-use terms separately

Keeping a mineral interest and allowing use of the surface are separate parts of the discussion. The TREC addendum provides a seller election about surface-use rights. Its warning matters: that election does not change rights held by other mineral owners outside the transaction, including existing lessees. Do not promise a buyer that your election alone means nobody can ever enter or use the property.

If an easement is also identified, keep its documents in the same review packet without assuming it is the same right. Our [Houston easement guide](/blog/selling-houston-house-easement/) covers that separate records check.

## Compare offers on the same rights

**Hypothetical:** One proposal pays $205,000 and another pays $210,000, but the second asks for an additional mineral interest. The $5,000 difference does not by itself show which proposal is better. First establish what you own and what each contract includes; this example assigns no value to minerals or future royalties.

Listing may expose the house to more buyers and produce a higher price if you can accommodate the process. A direct cash buyer may simplify preparation and financing, but can offer less and still need the title questions resolved. Neither route determines mineral ownership.
