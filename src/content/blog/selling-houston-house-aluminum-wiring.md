**If aluminum wiring is suspected in a Houston house you want to sell, obtain a qualified electrical assessment and gather records before deciding whether to remediate, rewire or seek a current-condition offer.** Do not assume the house needs a complete rewire from its age alone, or that a new panel proves every relevant connection was addressed.

The seller's useful job is to assemble a clear record of what is known. Diagnosing wiring and selecting a repair method belongs with a qualified electrician. Keep sale planning separate from any immediate safety response.

## Identify the actual wiring issue

The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission's [Repairing Aluminum Wiring publication](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/AL_0.pdf), published in June 2011, discusses overheating hazards associated with certain aluminum branch-circuit connections. It distinguishes that subject from larger aluminum conductors and copper-clad aluminum. The booklet remains publicly available from CPSC; its historical findings are not a current appraisal of your house.

Ask the electrician to identify the relevant conductor type, affected circuits, observed condition and recommended next steps in writing. A general phrase such as “older wiring” is not a complete diagnosis. Neither is an online photograph of a similar house.

Do not remove covers or disturb connections to produce photos for an offer request. If you notice an active burning smell, smoke or other immediate danger, prioritize appropriate emergency assistance. Selling plans should not delay a necessary safety response.

## Check what earlier work actually included

Locate electrical invoices, contractor details, permits where applicable, inspection records and any written remediation scope. Read the work description rather than relying on the invoice total or the phrase “updated electrical.”

A replacement panel and new kitchen fixtures might be legitimate improvements while leaving other branch-circuit connections untouched. Ask the prior contractor or a qualified current electrician which locations were included and what evidence supports completion. When records are missing, label the work unverified instead of calling the system fully remediated.

Keep the original documents and share relevant copies through an appropriate sale process. Avoid posting account information, private identification or unnecessary personal details publicly.

## Get comparable repair proposals

CPSC recommends qualified professional remediation when the wiring covered by its publication has not been remediated. Its booklet explains specific permanent approaches; this seller guide is not an installation manual. Ask the electrician to explain the proposed method, coverage and exclusions rather than requesting the cheapest generic “pigtail” job.

The [Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation's electrician page](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/electricians/) provides license verification and explains that non-exempt electrical work requires licensing through an electrical contractor. Confirm local permit requirements with the governing authority as well.

For each proposal, ask what is included, how access and wall repairs are handled, how the work will be documented, and what could change the schedule or price. Compare equivalent scope. A narrow correction quote should not be compared with a whole-house project as though they solve the same problem.

## Choose a sale path using the complete budget

Repairing before listing may make the property easier for some buyers to evaluate, but the return depends on price, other condition issues, costs and market response. A current-condition sale may reduce the seller's project-management burden while the offer accounts for expected work and uncertainty.

Use a hypothetical example: one seller has a documented limited concern, while another has no reliable wiring records and several unfinished renovations. They should not use the same assumed electrical allowance. Each needs an assessment appropriate to the house and a comparison of complete sale options.
