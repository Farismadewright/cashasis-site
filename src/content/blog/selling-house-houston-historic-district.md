**Before renovating a house in a Houston historic district for sale, verify its actual designation and ask about the proposed exterior work.** Do not spend money on replacement windows, siding or an addition merely because someone says the work will make the property easier to sell.

A selling decision and a construction decision are different. You can compare a sale in the property's current condition with a preparation plan while the relevant professionals clarify what work is permitted. This guide does not determine your property's legal status or approve a project.

## Verify designation instead of judging the house by age

The City's [Houston Office of Preservation project guide](https://www.houstontx.gov/planning/HistoricPres/plan_project.html) starts with designation status. It points to district maps and property classifications, including contributing and noncontributing structures, as well as landmark questions. Staff can help identify the classification.

A familiar neighborhood name, attractive porch or old construction date is not the same as a verified designation. Save the official information you find and the date checked. If a boundary or property match is unclear, ask for clarification before describing the house to a buyer.

This is City of Houston guidance. Do not apply it automatically to every Harris County address or another municipality's historic program.

## Define the work before requesting a price

The City's [Certificate of Appropriateness guidance](https://houstontx.gov/planning/HistoricPres/certofapp.html) explains preservation review for projects that change designated properties' exterior appearance. It distinguishes ordinary maintenance from alterations and notes that other permits may still apply. Contact staff about the actual scope.

Instead of asking a contractor for “a quick refresh,” write down the proposed change: repair an existing feature, replace material, change its appearance or build something new. Have the appropriate reviewer explain what applies before finalizing drawings or ordering materials.

Do not infer that a cash buyer's renovation idea has already been approved. A proposed sale price does not authorize demolition, relocation or exterior changes.

## Keep preservation records separate from general permits

Assemble available designation information, prior approvals, project descriptions and permit records. Note what was actually done and whether the records appear to match it. Do not edit old plans to make them appear current.

For a past addition, our [Houston permit-records guide](/blog/houston-house-unpermitted-addition-records/) addresses locating supporting information. Preservation review is an additional question; finding a general permit does not automatically answer it.

If you have received a notice or know of an unresolved project issue, give the actual documents to the responsible professional. Our [Houston code-violation guide](/blog/sell-house-code-violations-houston/) provides broader seller context. Neither guide determines a remedy, a fine or a property's compliance status.

## Compare the preparation plan with the sale plan

A hypothetical seller wants to replace several exterior features before listing. The first contractor estimate describes materials and labor but says nothing about approvals. That estimate is not yet a complete preparation budget or schedule.

Ask what review remains, who coordinates it and which parts of the budget depend on the answer. Then compare that defined plan with a current-condition purchase proposal. Include the effort of managing the project and the uncertainty of a later retail sale. Do not assume the proposed work increases the eventual price by more than it costs.

For larger condition issues, see our [Houston major-repair selling guide](/blog/sell-house-needs-major-repairs-houston/). Historic status is a reason to clarify the project, not a reason to invent a standard renovation allowance.

## Discuss the house without promising a future use

Listing may suit an owner seeking broad exposure to buyers who value the property and can accommodate its requirements. A Cash As-Is evaluation may suit an owner who prefers a current-condition discussion rather than managing optional presale work.

Share the condition, verified designation information and known unresolved questions when asking whether the house fits what we buy. We do not promise that every historic property qualifies or that an offer removes requirements. Compare the actual purchase terms and confirmed closing plan while keeping future work subject to its own review.

The useful outcome is a realistic choice about selling and preparation. It does not require guessing what the City will approve or changing original features before you understand the rules.
