**Before signing a Texas cash house offer, verify five things independently: who the buyer is, what entity will sign, how the purchase is funded, which licensed title or escrow agent will handle closing, and what the written contract actually requires.** No single certificate, bank letter or website proves a buyer will perform.

A legitimate buyer should be willing to explain the process and put material terms in writing. Verification protects the seller and also prevents misunderstandings with a real buyer.

## Match the person, company and contract name

Ask for the legal name that will appear as buyer. If the proposal uses a company, compare that exact name with the [Texas Comptroller Franchise Tax Account Status search](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/franchise/account-status/search). The Comptroller explains that this public status reflects an entity's right to transact business in Texas at the time of the search.

Treat that result as one identity check—not a reputation score. An active status does not establish available purchase funds or future performance. A mismatch may have an innocent explanation, such as a parent company or acquisition entity, but ask for it in writing before signing.

If a person claims to act as a real estate broker or sales agent, verify that professional claim through the Texas Real Estate Commission. A principal purchasing for its own account is not made trustworthy merely by using real-estate language, and not every principal buyer is acting as your representative.

## Request funding evidence that matches the proposal

Ask for current proof that reasonably supports the proposed price. The document might be a bank or brokerage letter, verified account evidence or a financing-source letter if the proposal is not truly funded from the buyer's own cash.

Check the date, amount or stated capacity, named holder and relationship to the contract buyer. The buyer may redact full account numbers and unrelated balances. If the name differs, ask how funds will move to the signing entity.

Proof of funds is evidence, not a guarantee. Ask whether the offer depends on inspection, partner approval, resale, outside financing or another event. Then make sure the written contract matches the explanation.

## Independently verify the closing channel

The [Texas Real Estate Commission's home buying and selling guide](https://www.trec.texas.gov/article/buying-and-selling-home-texas) describes a title or escrow agent as a neutral third party serving both sides and reviewing closing documents. It also notes that title and escrow agents are licensed through the Texas Department of Insurance, not TREC.

Use the [TDI title insurance FAQ](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/title/titlefaqs.html) to verify that the title company is licensed. Contact the office using a number you locate independently, not only a phone number pasted into an email or text. Confirm the address, closer, file-opening process and wiring instructions directly.

## Put the important offer terms side by side

| Check | Evidence to request | Limitation to remember |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer identity | Government ID or authorized signer details through the closing process | Do not post or circulate private ID copies unnecessarily |
| Entity | Exact legal name and current public status | Entity status does not prove funds or performance |
| Funding | Current proof tied to the proposed purchase | A letter does not guarantee the money will arrive |
| Contract | Price, property, closing, access, expenses, contingencies and assignment language | Verbal explanations do not replace written terms |
| Closing | Independently verified licensed title or escrow contact | A familiar-looking email address can still be spoofed |

</div>

Do not assume every cash contract uses the same earnest-money, option-fee, inspection or assignment structure. Cash As-Is wholesale agreements, for example, may use neither earnest money nor an option fee. Reliability should be judged from the entire written agreement, verified parties and demonstrated closing path—not one traditional-form feature.

If a provision is unclear, pause and have a Texas real estate attorney review it. This guide is a practical verification framework, not a legal opinion about a particular contract.

## Compare the offer, not just the buyer's slogan

Read who pays which costs, when access begins, what happens if title work finds a problem, whether the buyer may assign, the planned closing date, possession, personal-property handling and each party's termination rights. Ask the buyer to explain any open blank or cross-reference.

Then compare expected seller proceeds and responsibilities with realistic alternatives.
