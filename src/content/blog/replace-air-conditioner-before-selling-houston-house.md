**Do not replace a Houston house's air conditioner solely because you plan to sell. First establish what is wrong, what a repair or replacement would include, and whether that spending fits the sale you actually want.** An older working system and a failed system need different conversations.

A new AC may support a preparation-and-listing plan. An as-is proposal may let you compare another path before managing an installation. Neither choice is automatically the better financial result.

## Start with a diagnosis, not the equipment's age alone

[ENERGY STAR's replacement guidance](https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/heating-cooling/replace) identifies equipment age, frequent repairs, rising bills and uneven comfort as reasons to consider a professional review. It also notes that ductwork or insulation can contribute to comfort problems. That is a useful prompt to investigate, not a requirement to replace every older unit before selling.

Ask the contractor to distinguish an observed problem from a recommendation for an upgrade. Request the diagnosis in writing, including which part of the system was examined and what remains uncertain. If you obtain a second opinion, give that provider the same information.

For an initial offer conversation, describe actual behavior: whether the system runs, whether rooms cool evenly, and what repairs have been recommended. Do not describe the house as needing an entire new system merely because one estimate includes one.

## Compare like-for-like written scopes

A repair proposal and a replacement proposal can include different work. Before comparing their totals, ask what each one covers.

<div style="overflow-x:auto" role="region" aria-label="AC repair and replacement comparison for sellers" tabindex="0">

| Question | Why it matters to the sale decision |
|---|---|
| What specific problem was identified? | Separates a diagnosis from a general age-based recommendation |
| Which components are included? | Prevents comparing different installation scopes as if identical |
| What related work is excluded? | Identifies costs that could emerge after you commit |
| What warranty is actually offered? | Keeps written coverage separate from a verbal sales claim |
| When can the work be completed? | Helps you evaluate coordination and holding time |

</div>

Keep the equipment information, assessment and proposals together. A buyer can evaluate a clearer set of facts than a summary that simply says old AC. An estimate is still an estimate; do not turn it into proof that hidden or related issues have been resolved.

## Check an R-22 claim before treating it as a deadline

The [EPA's homeowner refrigerant FAQ](https://www.epa.gov/ods-phaseout/homeowners-and-consumers-frequently-asked-questions) says homeowners do not have to replace existing HCFC-22 equipment simply because of the phaseout. Existing equipment can continue to be serviced. This does not promise a particular repair will be economical or that refrigerant will be available at a particular price.

Ask a qualified technician to explain the refrigerant, parts and repair options for your system. EPA also advises using appropriately certified technicians and addressing a leak instead of repeatedly adding refrigerant. This guide does not provide refrigerant-handling instructions.

## Put the AC decision inside the whole-house comparison

Our [Houston major-repair guide](/blog/sell-house-needs-major-repairs-houston/) explains the broader current-condition selling option. Use the [repair-versus-selling tool](/fix-it-or-sell-it/) to organize your own assumptions rather than treating an equipment quote as the entire sale budget.

Compare the likely sale proceeds after preparation, selling costs and the time you expect to hold the property. Keep mortgage or other payoff items consistent across scenarios. If one route assumes additional improvements or a higher selling price, write those assumptions down instead of treating them as established facts.

Energy savings for a future occupant and resale proceeds for today's seller are different measurements. An efficiency claim does not demonstrate that replacing the AC will add the installation cost to a buyer's offer.
