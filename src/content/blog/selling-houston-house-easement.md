**Before deciding what an easement means for selling your Houston house, gather the actual easement document, the complete survey and a plain description of how that part of the property is used.** Ask the title professional, surveyor or your Texas real-estate attorney to review the relevant questions. The word “easement” alone does not tell you whether a sale can close or what work is needed.

That matters when you are comparing an as-is proposal with preparing the house for a listing. A label in old paperwork is a reason to ask a precise question, not automatically subtract a guessed amount from your price or start moving a fence.

## Separate the right, the drawing and today's use

[Texas A&M AgriLife Extension's Easements 101](https://mytexas.ag.tamu.edu/publications/easements-101/) explains that an easement gives rights to use land for a particular purpose rather than ownership of the land itself. For an express easement, its creating language is central to understanding those rights. Access and utility uses are examples to investigate, not assumptions about your parcel.

Keep three things separate in your working folder:

- **The document:** What instrument creates or describes the right, and which property does it concern?
- **The drawing:** What does the survey show, and what information was available when it was prepared?
- **The observed use:** What do you know about access, utilities, fences, driveways or other improvements today?

Your job as a seller is to provide what you know accurately. Determining the legal scope of the right or locating it on the ground is a professional review question. A neighbor's recollection, a fence or an online map should not become your unsupported promise about either.

## Start with a document packet

For a house in Houston, Harris County or Fort Bend County, use the records tied to the actual parcel and county. Keep the complete file rather than sending only a cropped line containing the word easement.

Gather what you already have:

1. The easement instrument and any later amendments or releases in your records.
2. The complete survey, including its date, notes and surveyor information.
3. Your previous title paperwork and the current transaction's title commitment when available.
4. Notes or photographs showing the area you are asking about, shared privately with the reviewer.
5. Any relevant notice or correspondence, with unanswered questions marked clearly.

If you only have a recording reference, ask the title team how to obtain the referenced instrument from the appropriate county records. Do not assume that a missing copy means the right no longer exists. Equally, a document you have not interpreted should not be described as proof of a violation.

## Ask what the title commitment is telling you

The [Texas Department of Insurance's title FAQ](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/title/titlefaqs.html) distinguishes the commitment before closing from the policy issued afterward. The commitment identifies issues and conditions for issuing coverage; it does not guarantee that every possible issue has been eliminated. TDI also explains that Schedule B exceptions concern items the policy does not cover.

Ask the title professional to connect the easement reference with its underlying document and explain the proposed insurance treatment. If a requirement or exception is unclear, ask what information is missing and whether a lawyer should review the legal question.

Do not treat every listed exception as an instruction to pay off, remove or cancel it. An easement is not simply another mortgage balance. Your review should identify the actual question before anyone assigns a repair budget or closing expectation to it. The broader [Houston title-problem guide](/blog/sell-house-title-problems-houston/) explains how property evaluation and title review can proceed alongside each other.

## Match the survey question to the right professional

[Texas REALTORS' survey guidance](https://www.texasrealestate.com/magazine/september-october-2025/will-that-survey-work) distinguishes different survey types and notes that a boundary survey may not address the restrictions or Schedule B matters in a title commitment. Send the complete drawing for review rather than assuming every document called a survey answers the same questions.

Ask the surveyor what the drawing shows and whether additional work is needed to locate the relevant features. Ask your attorney what the easement language permits or restricts. Ask the title professional what the transaction needs for its coverage review. These are connected questions, but one person's answer should not be stretched into a guarantee about another person's work.

If the question is simply whether your old survey can be reused, our [existing-survey checklist](/blog/existing-survey-when-selling-texas-house/) covers that separate preparation step.

## A practical example: the side-yard shed

**This is a hypothetical situation, not a Cash As-Is seller's story.** Imagine finding an easement reference in your purchase paperwork while preparing a house with a side-yard shed for sale.

The useful next step is to identify the referenced document and have its location and terms compared with the survey and actual shed. It is not to assume the shed must be demolished, or tell a buyer there is definitely no issue because it has stood there for years.

Write down the unresolved question, the reviewer handling it and the information requested. Keep proposed work marked “not determined” until you have a reason to budget it. Avoid inventing a universal discount for the easement.

## Compare offers after identifying the responsibilities

A listed sale may provide broader exposure and a higher price, depending on the property and market. A current-condition cash proposal may reduce renovation and showing demands, but its price may be lower. Neither funding method automatically settles an easement question or guarantees a closing date.
