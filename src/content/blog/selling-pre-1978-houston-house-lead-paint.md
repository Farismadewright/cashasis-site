**For most Houston houses built before 1978, lead-paint disclosure belongs on the sale checklist—even when the buyer is paying cash.** Gather what you already know and the reports you have before deciding whether to spend money on testing or repairs.

Three separate questions matter: what information must be provided, what inspection opportunity applies, and what work you choose to undertake. Keeping them separate helps you compare proposals without buying unnecessary work or overlooking paperwork.

## Check the age and the applicable rule first

The [EPA's lead-paint disclosure guidance](https://www.epa.gov/lead/real-estate-disclosures-about-potential-lead-hazards) covers most housing built before 1978, with specified exceptions. Do not assume that an as-is agreement or cash payment establishes an exception. Ask your agent or real estate attorney to confirm the requirements for the actual transaction.

A construction-year record is a starting point for that review, not a lead test. Likewise, fresh paint is not evidence that an older house has been certified lead-free. Keep the home's age, its current appearance and any professional findings in different columns of your working notes.

Our [Texas seller-disclosure checklist](/blog/texas-as-is-sale-seller-disclosure-checklist/) addresses the broader state condition notice. The federal lead packet deserves its own review rather than a checkmark beside a different form.

## Assemble the packet before signing

For covered housing, EPA identifies a buyer information pamphlet, known lead information, available records and reports, and a warning/compliance statement in or attached to the contract. It also tells sellers to retain the signed disclosures for three years after completion.

Use a private folder with a short inventory. This worksheet organizes the conversation; it is not a substitute for the required documents.

If you cannot find an old report, identify that gap. Do not write that the property passed an inspection because someone remembers a contractor visiting. Ask the professional who produced the document whether a copy can be obtained.

## Testing and disclosure are different decisions

EPA's [seller testing FAQ](https://www.epa.gov/lead/must-i-check-my-house-lead-prior-sale) says the disclosure rule does not require sellers to conduct or finance a lead inspection or risk assessment. That does not remove the information requirements or answer every other obligation concerning the property.

Before ordering a service solely to prepare for sale, ask what question the service will answer and who needs that answer. A buyer's request, a disclosure requirement and a seller's renovation plan may involve different documents and decisions.

Avoid treating a general painting invoice as a specialist assessment. If you are considering work, ask a qualified professional how to address the actual surfaces and applicable requirements. This guide does not provide a do-it-yourself lead-removal method.

## Put the buyer's inspection opportunity in writing

EPA describes a ten-day opportunity for a buyer's lead inspection or risk assessment. Its [guidance on changing that period](https://www.epa.gov/lead/can-inspectionrisk-assessment-period-testing-house-lead-be-waived) explains that mutual written consent can lengthen, shorten or waive it.

Have the relevant wording reviewed before choosing a closing date. Do not infer the buyer's decision from a conversation about speed, and do not confuse this federal opportunity with other provisions someone may use in a different contract.

## Compare the sale choices using the same information

An older house may suit a listing strategy, an as-is direct purchase, or preparation followed by marketing. A listing can reach more buyers and may produce a higher price; it can also require more coordination. A direct offer may reduce the preparation you manage, while its price reflects condition and the buyer's plan.
