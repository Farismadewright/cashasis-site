**You can explore selling a Houston house with a neglected pool before committing to a full restoration. Start by separating immediate safety concerns, the pool's actual condition and the cost of a defined repair or removal plan.** Then compare the expected proceeds and responsibilities of selling as-is with preparing the property for a wider buyer pool.

Green water, a nonworking pump and damaged coping are different problems. A single cleanup quote may not answer whether the shell, buried lines or equipment need more work. The useful first question is not “How much does every bad pool cost?” It is “What do we know about this pool, and what remains untested?”

## Make the assessment safe and specific

Keep access controlled and have appropriate professionals address safety concerns. Do not enter dirty water, work on electrical equipment or drain a pool merely to make it easier to photograph. Ask the pool professional what access and operating conditions are needed for a meaningful assessment.

Gather existing service invoices, equipment information, repair proposals, leak-test results and any notices from the applicable authority or homeowners association. Note when the pool last operated normally, which components were working and what has changed since. Distinguish what you observed from what a contractor actually diagnosed.

For a vacant property, include the pool in your ongoing property-management plan while the sale is being considered. An offer conversation does not transfer responsibility for the property. Our [vacant-house insurance checklist](/blog/vacant-house-insurance-while-selling-houston/) explains why occupancy and coverage questions deserve their own review.

## Ask what the inspection covers

Under the [Texas Real Estate Commission's inspection standards](https://www.trec.texas.gov/node/1948), pools and spas are optional systems. Confirm that your inspector agreed to include the pool. When included, the minimum standards address visible equipment and certain safety deficiencies, but do not require finding pool-shell or subsurface leaks or inspecting a system that is shut down or secured.

That limit matters when you compare proposals. “Pool inspected” does not tell you whether someone pressure-tested buried lines, evaluated a suspected leak or priced electrical corrections. Ask for the written scope and exclusions. A specialist's additional testing may answer a different question from the general inspection.

## Compare three written paths

| Path | What to put in writing | Main uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| Restore before marketing | Diagnosis, included repairs, exclusions, schedule and completion evidence | Hidden work or delays may change the budget |
| Remove the pool | Approved scope, local requirements, utility handling, grading and records | Removal may be more involved than filling the visible opening |
| Sell in current condition | Known defects, access, agreed responsibilities and actual offer terms | Buyers may allow for unresolved work and uncertainty |

</div>

If removal is under consideration, contact the authority that actually governs the property. A Houston mailing address alone does not establish City jurisdiction. Ask about applicable demolition, disconnection, fill and grading requirements before signing a contract. Retain the completed-work records for the next owner.

## Do not compare a firm offer with an unfinished repair estimate

Build both options from the same starting point. For a repair-and-list plan, account for the defined pool work, other preparation, selling expenses and the time you expect to carry the house. For an as-is proposal, read the price, expenses, access, possession, contingencies and closing responsibilities.

Here is a hypothetical decision example without a promised dollar result: a seller receives a low cleanup estimate, but it excludes a suspected underground leak. Treat the leak as unresolved rather than recording the cleanup quote as the total pool budget. Request an appropriate scope or compare an offer that expressly accounts for the current condition. The better choice depends on the resulting numbers, available cash and tolerance for managing work.

A restored pool may appeal to some retail buyers. Other buyers may prefer less maintenance. Neither response supports a universal claim that repair pays for itself or that cash always produces a better financial result. ## Give Cash As-Is the same clear condition picture

Share safe current photos, known defects, available inspection records and any notices. Explain whether the house is occupied and whether equipment can be operated safely by the appropriate professional. Do not promise a pool is leak-free based only on clear water or a working pump.

Compare the written offer with a realistic repair plan, and choose the route that fits your funds, timing and willingness to coordinate contractors.
