**A sewer-line problem does not automatically prevent a Houston house sale, but guessing about the location or repair can create avoidable confusion.** Identify the symptom, verify the likely public-versus-private boundary, keep the inspection evidence and compare a repair with listing or selling in current condition.

An as-is cash buyer may accept the condition and plan its own work. A traditional buyer may want a clearer scope, repair or concession. The useful comparison starts with evidence, not the assumption that every backup requires a full replacement.

## Record the symptom before diagnosing the line

Write down what happened and where: one slow fixture, several backed-up drains, wastewater at a cleanout, repeated stoppages, a yard depression or an odor. Add dates, photos and prior plumbing invoices. Do not describe the whole line as collapsed unless a qualified professional actually found that condition.

If wastewater is entering the house, protect people from exposure and obtain appropriate help promptly. Sale planning comes after immediate health and property protection.

## Verify who may be responsible for the segment

[Houston Public Works guidance](https://www.houstonpublicworks.org/protect-our-pipes) distinguishes public pipes or facilities in the public right-of-way from a blockage on a private line. Its cleanout guidance says that when no sewer water is standing by the cleanout, the blockage may be on the private side and a certified plumber should be contacted.

That is City of Houston guidance, not a boundary rule for every Houston mailing address. A house may be served by another municipality, a municipal utility district, a private system or a septic system. Ask the actual service provider and plumber to identify the relevant segment. ## Build a seller packet that another person can review

<div style="overflow-x:auto" role="region" aria-label="Sewer-line seller evidence packet" tabindex="0">

| Item | What it can clarify | What it does not automatically prove |
|---|---|---|
| Service-provider ticket | Reported public-side issue and response | Condition of the entire private line |
| Camera video and locator notes | Observed interior condition and estimated location | Final excavation access or total project price |
| Written plumbing estimate | Proposed scope, exclusions and price | Hidden conditions outside that scope |
| Prior invoice or warranty | Work performed and stated coverage | That every new symptom is covered |
| Seller disclosure | Seller's present knowledge | A buyer inspection or engineering warranty |

</div>

Ask for the actual video file and a written explanation of the material, defect, distance and access point. If roots, an offset joint or a break is reported, ask how the finding was located and what part of the line was visible.

## Compare repair, disclosure and current-condition offers

A repair may make sense when the scope is narrow, access is straightforward, the contractor and permit path are clear, and your sale timeline can absorb the work. A listing without repair may preserve market exposure but invite inspection negotiations or financing concerns. A current-condition sale may reduce coordination while producing a price that reflects the buyer's anticipated work and uncertainty.

Do not compare only headline prices. Put the proposed repair, carrying time, agreed seller expenses and buyer responsibilities beside each written offer. Our [major-repairs selling guide](/blog/sell-house-needs-major-repairs-houston/) explains that tradeoff across other systems too.

A simple hypothetical shows why scope matters: one proposal might price spot access at a known break, while another assumes replacement from the house to the connection. Those are different projects, not competing prices for the same work. Label the assumptions before using either figure in your sale decision.

## Keep disclosure and testing expectations in writing

The [Texas Real Estate Commission's Seller's Disclosure Notice page](https://www.trec.texas.gov/forms/sellers-disclosure-notice) states that the form concerns material facts and the physical condition of previously occupied single-family property. Requirements and exemptions depend on the transaction, so obtain appropriate advice and do not rely on the words as-is to erase known facts.

If a buyer wants a camera inspection, hydrostatic test or excavation, put access, restoration, authorization and responsibility in writing. TREC's rules identify sewer cameras as specialized inspection tools and separately recognize an addendum when parties agree to hydrostatic testing. The contract and qualified professionals should control the actual testing plan.
