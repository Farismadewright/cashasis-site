**A Houston-area flood map shows mapped hazard information; it does not establish whether a particular house has flooded.** Before answering a buyer, separate the effective map, your actual knowledge of the property and the records supporting that history.

That distinction is useful even when the house is dry and looks ready to sell. A reassuring map screenshot cannot erase a known event, and a neighbor's experience does not prove what happened inside your house.

## Start with the right map and record its date

The [Harris County Flood Control District's mapping guidance](https://www.hcfcd.org/Resources/Interactive-Mapping-Tools/Harris-County-Flood-Education) explains that its Flood Education Mapping Tool displays mapped floodplain information but is not the effective Flood Insurance Rate Map, or FIRM. It directs users to FEMA's Map Service Center for the effective map and explains that a FIRMette is a printable portion of it.

Follow that official guidance for the property you are reviewing. Save the map identification, effective date shown and date you accessed it. If the address pin or boundary seems ambiguous, ask the relevant floodplain administrator to clarify rather than drawing a conclusion from a small screen.

This Harris County tool should not be presented as the authority for every house with a Houston-area mailing address. Confirm the property's jurisdiction and the appropriate official resource.

## Keep mapped risk separate from observed history

The [Flood Control District's FAQ](https://www.hcfcd.org/Resources/FAQ) explains that mapped floodplains estimate flooding from a bayou or creek under specified assumptions. It also describes local ponding and overland flow when intense rainfall exceeds street-drainage capacity.

The practical implication is that being outside a mapped high-risk area is not proof of no flooding. Nor does an old event, by itself, tell you the property's current mapped designation. Keep both pieces of information intact.

Avoid a sweeping statement such as no flood risk. A more useful conversation identifies the source, what it says and what you actually know. This guide is an evidence-organizing method, not an insurance quote, flood certification or property-specific legal opinion.

## Build a three-part property folder

Use separate tabs so a buyer can see which question each document answers.

<div style="overflow-x:auto" role="region" aria-label="Flood mapping and property history preparation" tabindex="0">

| Folder section | What belongs there | What it does not establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Map information | Dated official map, map identifier and any clarification received | Every event inside the house |
| Known property history | Your observations and available property-specific reports or photographs | A prediction of future flooding |
| Work and claim records | Actual repair, assessment and claim documents you possess | That every issue is resolved |

</div>

Keep the original documents and make a working list of gaps. If two records appear inconsistent, identify the exact point of disagreement. For example, a dated map and a photograph of water in a room are different kinds of evidence; neither should be discarded merely because they tell different parts of the story.

Do not copy a neighbor's dates or descriptions into your own property history. Likewise, distinguish water entering the building from water you only observed in the street or yard. Ask a qualified professional to help resolve uncertainty instead of making the account sound more definite.

## Ask buyers to evaluate the same facts

A useful comparison gives each buyer or listing professional the same available information. Ask what remains to be assessed, what responsibilities the proposal places on you and how uncertainty affects the offer. Compare likely proceeds and work required alongside price.

Listing can reach a wider group of buyers and may produce a higher price. A Cash As-Is evaluation can be practical when you prefer to discuss the house in its current condition and avoid managing a retail preparation project. Neither approach guarantees a particular outcome or makes flood information irrelevant.
