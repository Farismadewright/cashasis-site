**Earnest money and an option fee are different parts of a Texas house-sale agreement—even when the buyer offers cash.** Before judging how dependable an offer is, identify the amounts, who receives them and what termination provisions the actual contract contains.

A large proposed deposit is not a guarantee of closing. A smaller one is not, by itself, proof of a bad buyer. The useful question is whether the written terms, verified receipt and purchase plan fit your needs.

This general seller guide uses the current TREC resale form as a reference. Your agreement may differ. It is not advice to cancel a contract, keep a deposit or calculate a legal deadline.

## Start with the contract, not the word cash

[TREC Form 20-19](https://www.trec.texas.gov/forms/one-four-family-residential-contract-resale), effective July 1, 2026, separates earnest money and the option fee in paragraph 5. Under that form, both are payable to the named escrow agent. The option fee concerns a negotiated unrestricted termination right; earnest money has a separate role in the transaction.

The [current contract PDF](https://www.trec.texas.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-forms/20-19_4.pdf) explains that the option fee is credited toward the sales price at closing. It also describes different consequences for option termination, late payment and other contract events. Do not replace those provisions with a general assumption that every deposit is nonrefundable.

For a broader explanation of the purchase process, read [how a Houston cash offer works](/blog/how-cash-offer-house-works-houston/). This guide focuses specifically on checking the money and terms after a proposal arrives.

## Put five questions beside the offer price

Use this review sheet before you compare proposals. Fill it from the actual documents and verified closing-office information—not from what someone says is standard.

| Question | What to write down |
|---|---|
| What is identified as earnest money? | Amount and named recipient |
| Is an option fee and period included? | Amount, terms and where they appear |
| Who confirms receipt? | Independently verified escrow contact |
| What conditions remain? | Questions for the contract reviewer |
| What supports the closing plan? | Information still needed from the buyer |

A blank or unclear answer is a follow-up task, not proof that you have no rights or that the sale is invalid. Ask your representative or Texas real-estate attorney to explain unclear language before you rely on it.

## Verify receipt without confusing it with funding

Contact the named escrow office through contact information you independently trust. Ask whether the expected funds were received and whether anything remains outstanding. Keep the confirmation with the contract file.

Separate that question from whether the buyer can fund the entire purchase. Confirmation of a deposit answers a deposit question; it does not establish every other part of the buyer's financing or purchase plan. A payment screenshot, an optimistic text and a verified escrow receipt are not interchangeable pieces of evidence.

For safe communication practices, see our [sale-proceeds wire-fraud checklist](/blog/protect-house-sale-proceeds-wire-fraud/). Never send banking details in response to an unexpected message merely because it refers to your transaction.

## Review the exit terms before relying on the deposit

TREC's [seller FAQ about an unpaid option fee](https://www.trec.texas.gov/node/368) explains that failure to timely deliver the fee affects the unrestricted option right. The FAQ references an older form number, so use the current signed form and legal advice for your transaction. Loss of one option right should not be treated as proof that every possible termination right disappears.

If payment is missing, or a buyer wants to terminate, do not use this article as a cancellation notice or instruction to spend the funds. Ask the appropriate professional to review the agreement, communications and release procedure. Timing and notice questions can change the result.

## Compare two offers as plans, not just numbers

Imagine two hypothetical proposals with the same purchase price. One provides clear written responsibilities and a verifiable closing contact. The other leaves the payment recipient and remaining conditions unclear. The sensible next step is to resolve the second proposal's unknowns—not to assume the deposit figure settles the comparison.
