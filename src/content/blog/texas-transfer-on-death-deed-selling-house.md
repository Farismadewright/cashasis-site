**If you find a Texas transfer-on-death deed while preparing a Houston-area house for sale, locate the recorded instrument and have the ownership questions reviewed before signing as the seller.** A paper in a family folder, a recorded deed and confirmed authority for a particular sale are different stages of the review.

This guide concerns an existing deed someone has found. It does not provide instructions for creating an estate plan, determine who owns your property or promise that probate is unnecessary in your situation.

## Identify the document you actually found

The [Texas State Law Library's transfer-after-death guide](https://guides.sll.texas.gov/wills-and-directives/transfer-property-after-death) explains transfer-on-death deeds and directs readers to the county clerk for existing recorded instruments. It describes a way property can pass directly after death, rather than treating every inherited-property situation as the same process.

Check the document's title and locate the complete pages. Do not call every deed naming a family member a transfer-on-death deed, and do not substitute a will, power of attorney or affidavit of heirship for the document actually under review.

For a house in Harris County or Fort Bend County, confirm where the property is located before requesting records. A Houston mailing address alone does not answer that county-records question.

## Recording and timing deserve an early check

[Texas Estates Code Chapter 114](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/ES/htm/ES.114.htm) authorizes this type of transfer to take effect at the transferor's death. Section 114.055 includes recording before that death among the requirements for effectiveness. The chapter also permits revocation and describes the transfer as subject to existing interests and encumbrances.

Those rules explain why a reviewer needs more than a remembered family intention. They do not establish that a deed you found was effective, identify the current owner or settle competing claims. Have a qualified Texas attorney and the title team evaluate the actual instruments and circumstances.

Ask whether the file contains the recorded version, whether the legal description matches the property being discussed, and whether another document changes the picture. If you do not know, say so. Do not try to repair a missing recording history by altering, backdating or assuming the meaning of a document.

## Prepare a focused review packet

Start with what you have and ask what additional evidence is needed:

- The complete recorded deed and its recording reference, if available.
- Prior deeds or title paperwork identifying the property.
- Any later deeds, revocations or other instruments already in your files.
- Death documentation and beneficiary information requested through an appropriate private channel.
- Known mortgage, tax, lien or co-owner questions affecting the proposed sale.

This is an organizational list, not a universal list of documents sufficient to close. Ask the reviewer to distinguish a missing document from a disputed legal question. Identify who is handling each outstanding item rather than assuming a buyer will resolve everything automatically.

## Keep the document question separate from other inheritance routes

An affidavit of heirship and a transfer-on-death deed are not interchangeable labels. Do not order one simply because an online article says it can make a sale quicker. Give the professionals the existing recorded instruments and ask which process, if any, fits the property.

Likewise, do not assume that avoiding one procedure makes the property free of obligations. A title review still needs to address the interests relevant to the proposed transfer. An offer price should not be presented as money every beneficiary can immediately divide before ownership and closing deductions have been reviewed.

## A useful next-step conversation

**Hypothetical example:** A family finds a signed deed in a drawer after the owner's death and wants to accept an offer. Instead of treating the signature as proof of a completed transfer, they ask the title team to locate the recorded instrument and their attorney to evaluate its effect. The review may reveal additional questions; this example promises no particular result.

Keep a short list: what was found, what has been verified, what remains unknown and who is answering it. Do not give a buyer or another family member a firm closing promise while a material ownership issue remains unresolved.

## Compare the property sale after clarifying responsibilities

Listing an inherited house may provide wider exposure and a higher price, particularly when the property can be prepared for the market. An as-is cash offer may reduce renovation, cleanout or showing demands but can be lower. Neither choice determines the effect of the deed or guarantees a title outcome.
