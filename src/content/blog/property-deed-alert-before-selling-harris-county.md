**An unexpected property alert is a reason to verify the recorded document before making sale promises, not proof by itself that your house was stolen.** If the document remains unfamiliar, get qualified review rather than guessing what it means for your sale.

For a Harris County seller, the useful first question is precise: what document was recorded, does it concern this property, and who is reviewing the unresolved issue? Keep that question separate from the house's condition or the price a buyer proposes.

## Understand the notice before reacting to it

The [Harris County Clerk's Property Deed Fraud guidance](https://cclerk.hctx.net/PropertyDeedFraud.aspx) explains that its free Property Alert System monitors names. A name match can concern someone else's property. The system neither blocks recording nor determines ownership. The Clerk advises prompt document review and legal advice when fraud is suspected.

Do not turn an alert into your own title conclusion. Equally, do not dismiss an unfamiliar document merely because you have lived in the house for years. Your next step is verification, with the actual record in front of the appropriate reviewer.

## Reach the records through a trusted route

If the message is unexpected, use an independently verified county website or contact rather than assuming every link carrying an official-looking name is genuine. The [Federal Trade Commission's phishing guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-avoid-phishing-scams) recommends contacting an organization through a website or number you know is real when checking a suspicious message.

Keep a copy of the notice and note the recording reference it identifies. If you cannot locate the document, ask the Clerk's records staff for help accessing it. Keep requests for passwords, payment or identity documents out of the response until you have verified who is asking and why.

Our [seller wire-fraud checklist](/blog/protect-house-sale-proceeds-wire-fraud/) covers the separate question of changes to closing-payment instructions. A document alert and a payment message deserve different checks.

## Build a small review packet

Organize the question before asking whether the sale can close. A practical packet can include:

- The complete notice and the recorded document it references.
- Your deed and relevant purchase or closing paperwork already in your records.
- The recording number and date shown on the document, without substituting a guessed description.
- A short note separating what you recognize from what you cannot explain.
- The proposed sale timeline, clearly marked as a plan rather than a promise.

Share this privately through the reviewer's verified channel. Posting an unredacted document publicly to ask strangers who owns the property creates another problem without settling the first one.

Ask for a concrete handoff: who is reviewing the document, what additional information is needed, and when will you receive an update? Keep an unanswered question labeled unresolved. Do not describe the packet itself as proof that title is clear.

## Separate the closing review from any insurance question

The [Texas Department of Insurance's title FAQ](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/title/titlefaqs.html) explains that a title commitment identifies potential issues and conditions; it does not guarantee the absence of problems. TDI also distinguishes title coverage from homeowners insurance and describes limits on coverage for later-created defects.

Have the closing team explain the transaction question and your attorney address disputed rights. If you have an existing title policy, ask its underwriter about the applicable notice and claim process instead of assuming a new suspicious filing must be covered. Do not choose a legal filing or promise a cure based on a general seller article.

## Compare selling routes with the unresolved issue visible

Listing can provide broader exposure and may produce a higher price if you can accommodate preparation, showings and the sale process. A current-condition cash proposal may reduce those demands, but its price can be lower. Neither route answers a disputed ownership question or guarantees that a proposed closing date is achievable.

Before comparing offers, ask each buyer what the proposal assumes about title review, required documents and timing. Keep any unresolved item visible in the discussion instead of treating the highest price as an executable closing plan.
